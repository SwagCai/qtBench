# qtBench

qtBench is a benchmark of open problems in q,t-combinatorics aimed at frontier AI models.

Many questions in the area of algebraic combinatorics ask for combinatorial models of symmetric
functions, graded characters,
Hilbert series, or q,t-analogues that arise in algebra, geometry, and
representation theory.

qtBench turns these questions into three kinds of discovery tasks:

- [**q-statistic discovery**](docs/task_types.md#q_statistic_discovery): Find a natural statistic whose weighted enumeration realizes the given polynomials.
- [**t-statistic discovery**](docs/task_types.md#t-statistic-discovery): Discover the missing statistic(s) needed to realize the multivariate polynomials.
- [**bijection discovery**](docs/task_types.md#bijection-discovery): Construct a natural bijection that explains a given equality or symmetry on the combinatorial objects.

The benchmark contains 29 unsolved challenges and one solved calibration problem.
A statistic problem specifies the
objects and target polynomials, then asks for the missing statistic(s). A bijection problem asks for an explicit map explaining a symmetry
of a q,t-enumerator. Most of the problems come from open questions in the
literature; others concern algebraic formulas with no known direct combinatorial
interpretation. Problem `1` is already solved and it is included as an easy calibration test.

Each problem's directory includes a self-contained statement and references, the
combinatorial objects and public targets, and a tested evaluator interface.

## Why should you care?

### For AI researchers

Agents are becoming increasingly capable in mathematics, yet they still
struggle with certain classes of problems that require at the same time creativity and rigor.
We believe that many problems related to discovering combinatorial statistics and bijections fall into such classes, even if, at first sight, they may seem unusually amenable to AI-based approaches.

In fact, there are several reasons to believe that agents should be better than
humans at these problems:

- **Candidate solutions are easy to verify.** A proposed statistic can be
  tested in seconds, without requiring a Lean proof or any external verifier.
  Propose a function, run it on the combinatorial objects, and check whether it
  gives the right polynomial: easy peasy.
- **Agents can explore much faster than humans.** They can propose, modify, and
  test far more candidate functions than any human mathematician could in the
  same amount of time. Because part of the problem is exploring a large space
  of possible statistics or bijections, this is a natural advantage for agents.
- **No advanced or obscure theory is required.** These are not problems where
  the crucial insight depends on an advanced theorem or on mathematics that was
  never written down and exists only in the prefrontal cortex of a handful of
  experts. Everything the agent needs is right there: the combinatorial
  objects, the target polynomials, and the rules of the game. The surrounding
  mathematics can be deep, but the solution is often elementary.

These reasons suggest that agents could achieve superhuman performance on this
class of problems. So why are they not there yet?

We have two compatible hypotheses. The first is that these problems provide
almost no useful notion of distance from a solution. One can certainly define
proxy measures. In statistic discovery, for example, one can compare the
polynomial generated by a proposed statistic with the target polynomial and
quantify the difference. But this is usually a poor proxy for real *semantic*
progress. A statistic whose distribution is extremely close to the target may
still be conceptually far from a correct solution, while the right next step is
often to abandon the current idea and switch to a completely different feature
of the combinatorial objects. Here, being close by some proxy does not mean
being close to a solution.

Our second hypothesis is that solving these problems requires some form of
creativity. The meta-problem is always the same—find a statistic or find a
bijection—but the individual problems can be completely unrelated. Knowing the
solution to one problem often provides little information about how to solve
the next one. There is no general method for statistic discovery, no standard
sequence of moves, and no known procedure that reliably turns a target
polynomial into the desired combinatorial rule.

What humans do is much less systematic: they inspect the objects and
polynomials, compute examples, notice patterns, discard them, and gradually
develop an intuition for which structural features might matter.

A useful analogy is a child playing with a shape-sorting toy. A sphere will not
fit into the square hole, even if it comes very close. The child has to
manipulate the objects, try different orientations, fail repeatedly, and
eventually understand which shapes fit where. We view statistic and bijection
discovery in a similar way: prolonged play with examples must somehow produce
some form of intuition.

For these reasons, we think making agents good at these problems is an
interesting challenge in its own right. This is not only because the benchmark
contains difficult and mathematically important open problems—some of them
famous and decades old, such as the
[area-bounce exchanging bijection](problems/exchanging_bijection/dyck_area_bounce_exchange/problem.md).
We also believe that progress on these tasks could improve capabilities that
matter beyond algebraic combinatorics: coming up with new ideas, recovering
when a line of attack is not working, and tracking a problem over long stretches
of reasoning without getting stuck in local patterns.

Our bet is that making agents better at these tasks will both help us
understand creativity and long-horizon reasoning and bring real value to the
mathematical community.

### For mathematicians

If you work in $q,t$-combinatorics, or in algebraic combinatorics more broadly,
and part of your research involves searching for statistics, bijections, or
combinatorial interpretations, you have probably noticed that existing
automated systems are still quite limited in how much they can help. You can ask
an LLM for a solution or experiment with symbolic and computational tools, but
current systems rarely solve the given problems.

We have reasons to believe that statistic and bijection discovery could be at
least partially automated, and this motivates our work. In [previous work](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19063) we
explored other approaches to automated statistic and bijection discovery. Those
methods showed that non-trivial automated discovery is possible, but also that
the approaches do not scale well. Our current view is that achieving automated
discovery at scale will probably require combining large language models with
appropriate tools.

qtBench was created with this goal in mind: to provide a benchmark for
evaluating how current models and harnesses perform on these problems. We also
plan to share it with research groups and companies working on state-of-the-art
models, in order to give them a concrete target to optimize toward.
We hope that our efforts will provide the means of measuring the progress 
clearly and consistently, and accelerate it.

If this direction interests you—whether because you have open problems that
would make good benchmark tasks, ideas about automated discovery, or simply
want to experiment with the problems—we would be very happy to hear from you or
to receive contributions to the benchmark.

### For everyone else

Not a mathematician or an AI researcher? No problem. If you are excited about
AI's incredible progress and have a swarm looking for trouble, point it at one
of our open problems. The worst it can do is burn some tokens, but if you are lucky, you may discover some new mathematics!

## Quick Start

qtBench requires Git and [UV](https://docs.astral.sh/uv/). The package requires
Python 3.11 or newer; the included `.python-version` selects Python 3.13, which
UV downloads automatically if needed.
If UV is not already installed, follow its
[installation instructions](https://docs.astral.sh/uv/getting-started/installation/).
Clone the repository and enter its directory:

```bash
git clone https://github.com/SwagCai/qtBench.git
cd qtBench
```

The examples below use POSIX `\` line continuations. In PowerShell, run each
multi-line command on one line.

Create the locked base environment, then score the known-good submission for
problem `1`, the benchmark's solved calibration problem:

```bash
uv sync --locked
```

```bash
uv run --locked python scripts/evaluate/evaluate_scored_submission.py \
  noncrossing examples/noncrossing_calibration_submission.py
```

A working setup ends with `PASSED` and reports that the marginal, full target,
replay, value audit, and resource checks all pass.

To see how the evaluator reports an incorrect candidate, run:

```bash
uv run --locked python scripts/evaluate/evaluate_scored_submission.py \
  noncrossing examples/constant_zero_submission.py
```

This command is expected to report `did not pass (stopped at: numerical)` and
exit with status `1`; it demonstrates a numerical rejection, not an installation
failure. To try your own statistic, copy that short template, edit its
`statistic` function, and pass your file to the same command. For other
problems, use the evaluator kind in [`docs/problems.md`](docs/problems.md) and
the submission contract in the problem's `problem.md`.

To regenerate the public data or run the tests, install the development
dependencies:

```bash
uv sync --extra dev --locked
```

```bash
uv run --extra dev --locked pytest -q
```

The suite contains more than 650 tests and takes several minutes. It covers the
checker plus problem-specific consistency and reproducibility checks. See
[`docs/data_generation.md`](docs/data_generation.md) for the generation commands,
independent derivations, and documented exceptions.

## Submission API

A submission is a short, self-contained Python file. Statistic problems define
`statistic`; bijection problems define `forward` and `inverse`. Each problem
statement gives the exact argument and return types. For problem `1`, the API is:

```python
def statistic(partition) -> int:
    ...
```

Here `partition` is a `qtbench.combinatorics.NoncrossingPartition`. Its public
interface includes `n`, `blocks`, `blocks_by_max`, `block_maxima`,
`block_sizes_by_max`, `nonmaximal_elements`, and `area()`.

A statistic submission must pass capability screening, source-economy limits,
exact numerical checks, fresh-namespace replay, an integer-value audit, and a
resource gate on large objects. For bijections, the checker repeatedly verifies
the forward and inverse identities pointwise. Passing is necessary but not
sufficient for a mathematical solution; see [`docs/checker.md`](docs/checker.md)
and the attack catalogue in
[`docs/cheating/taxonomy.md`](docs/cheating/taxonomy.md).

Run the scored evaluator against the public data with:

```bash
uv run --locked python scripts/evaluate/evaluate_scored_submission.py \
  noncrossing examples/constant_zero_submission.py
```

The default report covers the public one-variable marginal and the full
polynomial check. Add `--format json` for the complete result, case by case. The
explicit marginal uses the conventional filename `data/q_equals_1.json`; when
the grading variable is `u = q - 1`, this file records `u = 1`.

For a quick, non-scoring q=1 check during development:

```bash
uv run --locked python scripts/evaluate/evaluate_submission.py \
  problems/t_statistic_discovery/nc_area_qt_narayana_second_stat \
  examples/constant_zero_submission.py \
  --q-equals-1
```

`scripts/evaluate/evaluate_submission.py` is a Problem 1 maintainer tool for
inspecting coefficient differences. It rejects other problem directories; use
their evaluator kind with `evaluate_scored_submission.py`. The diagnostic is not
used for scoring because it loads ordinary, unrestricted Python.

## How to check if a statistic or a bijection is natural?

As we mentioned above, we are not looking for just **any** function that satisfies the constraints, but for one that is *natural*. There is no formal definition of naturalness, and no algorithmic checker can perfectly distinguish a genuine combinatorial construction from a sufficiently clever way of cheating. Our checker therefore has an asymmetric goal: **accept genuine natural solutions that conform to the submission contract while rejecting as many obvious cheating strategies as possible.** For this reason, passing the checker is necessary, but not sufficient, for a proposed solution to count as a genuine mathematical discovery.

We maintain a [cheating taxonomy](docs/cheating/taxonomy.md) describing the shortcuts we know about and which ones can or cannot be detected mechanically. An optional **LLM judge** can provide an additional advisory review with the problem, proposed solution, and cheating taxonomy in context, but its verdict is non-authoritative. The next section explains how.

## AI judge

The optional AI judge asks a selected provider and model to flag possible
cheating. Install the matching UV extra and set its API-key environment variable:

| Provider | UV extra | API-key environment variable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| OpenAI | `ai-judge-openai` | `OPENAI_API_KEY` |
| Anthropic | `ai-judge-anthropic` | `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` |
| Gemini | `ai-judge-google` | `GEMINI_API_KEY` or `GOOGLE_API_KEY` |

Then run, for example:

```bash
uv sync --extra ai-judge-anthropic --locked
uv run --extra ai-judge-anthropic --locked qtbench-ai-judge \
  1 path/to/submission.py --provider anthropic --model MODEL_ID
```

Use `--prompt-for-credential` to enter the selected credential at a hidden TTY
prompt, or run `qtbench-ai-judge --help` for all command options.

The verdict is advisory and non-authoritative: it neither runs nor replaces the
deterministic checker, proves naturalness, nor provides a security guarantee.
For an attempted review, exit status `0` means that a structured advisory report
was produced, even when its overall risk level is `high` or it contains a
`critical` finding; it is not a passing verdict. Status `1` means the provider
request or response failed, and status `2` means command usage, local input,
credentials, SDK initialization, or configuration failed. The normal exception
is `--help`, which exits `0` after printing usage without producing a report.
See [`docs/ai_judge.md`](docs/ai_judge.md) for the technical protocol, privacy
considerations, and provider-adapter details.

## Layout

- `src/qtbench/`: reusable combinatorial objects and evaluator code. Coxeter families are grouped under `combinatorics/type_a/`, `combinatorics/type_b/`, and `combinatorics/type_d/`.
- `problems/`: statements, metadata, public targets, generators, and problem-specific oracles, organized by task type.
- `scripts/`: shared entry points for data generation and evaluation.
- `examples/`: scorer-compatible examples. The solved calibration example passes; the remaining files are interface templates, most of which stop at the numerical stage. The promotion example requires expert review because automatic scoring is disabled. See [`examples/README.md`](examples/README.md).
- `tests/`: checker regressions and, under `tests/problems/`, problem-specific consistency and reproduction checks.
- `docs/`: benchmark design and problem-authoring documentation.
- `docs/cheating/`: the threat model and taxonomy of cheating attempts.

## Reproducing the Public Data

Most problems have a public target-emission entry point beside their statement,
and a derivation oracle is included when available. Problems `4` and `5` instead
share `scripts/generate/generate_polyomino_data.py`. The generators use exact
integer or rational arithmetic unless the documentation specifies an additional
certification step. Problem `15` includes a SageMath script certifying every
scored rank over `QQ`. Problem `3` is a documented committed-target exception:
its entry point re-emits aggregate target records, while the direct-rank and
conditional-reconstruction derivation pipeline is not included.
The scored evaluator reads only metadata and public target JSON, never a
generator or oracle. Reproduction commands and exceptional cases are documented
in [`docs/data_generation.md`](docs/data_generation.md).
